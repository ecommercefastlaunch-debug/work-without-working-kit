# Breakthrough Advertising: Core Principles

A working summary of Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), adapted for modern ads (video, social, digital).

## 1. The three questions before any headline

Before writing a single word, answer:
1. **What mass desire is this product channeling?**
2. **How aware is the prospect** of their problem, the solution, and this product? (State of Awareness)
3. **How many similar products and claims has the prospect already heard?** (State of Sophistication)

These answers decide the hook, the claim, and the structure. Schwartz's point: copy is not "written" from inspiration. It is *assembled* from the desire, awareness, and sophistication of the market.

## 2. Mass desire

- **Copy cannot create desire.** It can only channel desires that already exist in large numbers of people onto a specific product. Creating new desire is education, and education is too slow and expensive for advertising.
- Desire comes from two sources:
  - **Permanent forces / mass instincts:** health, looks, love, money, status, security, comfort, being admired, avoiding pain or fear. These never go away.
  - **Forces of change:** new problems, technologies, trends, fashions, and social shifts that create new desires (for example, remote work creating demand for home-office comfort, or rising prices creating demand to save money).
- Each desire has **three dimensions**:
  - **Urgency/intensity:** how badly they need it satisfied now
  - **Staying power:** how persistent it is (it keeps coming back)
  - **Scope:** how many people share it
- A product can satisfy many desires, but **one must dominate the ad**. Choosing the right desire is the single most important decision. The others can support it.
- The ad's job: **acknowledge the desire, strengthen it, and show the product as the means of satisfying it.**

## 3. The 5 levels of awareness

From most aware to least aware, with how to open the ad:

| Level | What the prospect knows | How the hook opens |
|---|---|---|
| **Most aware** | Knows the product, wants it, just needs the deal | The product name and the offer: price, discount, deadline, bonus. Don't get fancy. |
| **Product aware** | Knows the product but isn't convinced | Reinforce desire for it, prove superiority, sharpen the image, introduce a new mechanism or new proof, handle objections. |
| **Solution aware** | Knows the result they want, not that this product delivers it | Name the desire or result in the hook, prove it can be achieved, then show this product is how. |
| **Problem aware** | Feels the pain, doesn't know a solution exists | Name the problem and agitate it, then present the solution. Make them *feel* it before offering relief. |
| **Unaware** | Not consciously thinking about the problem | Never lead with the product or price. Lead with identification, a story, a surprising fact, or a truth about themselves that pulls them in. This is the hardest level. |

The less aware the prospect, the more the ad must do before the product can be mentioned, and the longer the copy tends to be.

## 4. The 5 stages of market sophistication

How many similar claims the market has heard determines what kind of claim will still work.

| Stage | Market condition | What to do | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | You're first; no one has made this claim | State the claim simply and directly, then dramatize it | "Finally: clear skin." |
| **2** | Competitors copy the claim | Enlarge the claim: bigger, faster, more specific, with proof | "Clear skin in 14 days, or your money back." |
| **3** | Claims are exhausted and no longer believed | Introduce a **new mechanism**, the *how* that makes the result possible | "The rice-water ferment that clears skin from the inside." |
| **4** | Competitors copy the mechanism | Enlarge the mechanism: make it better, easier, faster | "The first 2-minute rice-ferment routine, no harsh actives." |
| **5** | The market is tired of all claims and mechanisms | Shift to **identification**. Sell the identity and experience: who the buyer becomes and which tribe they join | "For women who are done hiding behind makeup." |

Most local and online small-business markets today are at **stage 3–5**. Everyone already says "high quality, affordable, effective." That is why mechanism and identification usually win.

## 5. The headline (hook)

- The headline's only job is to **stop the right prospect and make them read (or watch) the next line**. It isn't meant to sell the whole product.
- It should contain **the desire (or the problem) at the right awareness level**. When possible, add an image the reader can see, the mechanism, or a specific claim.
- Specificity creates belief: numbers, timeframes, names, places, sensory details.
- In video, the hook is the first 1–3 seconds of speech *plus* what's on screen. Both must work together.
- Write many hooks and keep the strongest. A small hook improvement can multiply results.

## 6. The three dimensions of the prospect

Schwartz describes three forces inside the prospect that copy works with:
- **Desire:** what they want (see mass desire). Intensify it.
- **Identification:** who they are and who they want to be (the roles they play, the status they seek). Let them see themselves in the ad.
- **Belief:** what they already accept as true. Never fight it head-on. Build on existing beliefs to lead them to new conclusions.

A great ad strengthens desire, mirrors identity, and uses existing belief as its foundation.

## 7. The 7 breakthrough copy techniques

### 1. Intensification
Make the desire and the promise feel bigger, more vivid, and more real.
- Show the product **in action**, and show the reader in the scene.
- Describe the results **in detail**: sensory, specific, and before versus after.
- **Compare** (vs. the old way, vs. competitors, vs. cost of doing nothing).
- **Dramatize** with stories, demonstrations, and scenarios.
- **Stack proof**: testimonials, numbers, authority, demonstrations, guarantees. Schwartz called this verification: making the claim believable.
- Order the claims so the ad climbs to a peak.

### 2. Identification
Let the prospect see themselves, or who they want to be, in the ad.
- **Character identification:** reflect the roles they play or aspire to ("the mom who always has it together", "the owner who leaves the office at 5").
- **Achievement identification:** show the accomplishment they'd be proud of and what others will think of them.
- Use their words, their setting, their daily moments.

### 3. Gradualization
Lead the reader through a **chain of statements they already accept**, one step at a time, until they arrive at a conclusion they would have rejected if you'd stated it upfront.
- Start with something obviously true ("We all know cheap bags don't last.")
- Each step follows naturally from the last.
- End at the product as the only logical answer.
- Essential for Unaware markets and for bold or unusual claims.

### 4. Redefinition
Remove an objection by **reframing** it, often before the prospect raises it.
- "It's not expensive, it's ₱16 a day: less than your coffee."
- "This isn't a diet. It's a way to eat your favorite foods differently."
- "Yes, it takes 10 minutes. That's why it actually works."
- Turn the weakness into proof of the strength.

### 5. Mechanization
Explain **how it works** in clear, concrete words so the result feels inevitable, not magical.
- Name the mechanism ("The 3-Layer Stitch", "The 60-Day HR Foundation Method").
- Give a simple cause → effect explanation.
- A believable mechanism is what wins sophisticated markets (stage 3–4).

### 6. Concentration
Make the case **against the alternatives**.
- Show why other solutions (competitors, DIY, cheap substitutes, doing nothing) fail or fall short, fairly and factually.
- Then show this product delivers exactly what they lack.
- Don't name competitors unless it is legal and wise. Name the *category* ("most online leather sellers…", "generic templates…").

### 7. Camouflage
Present the ad in a form that **doesn't look like an ad**, such as an article, a tip, news, a tutorial, or a personal story, so prospects who ignore ads still engage.
- Modern forms: "3 things I wish I knew about…", POV videos, a founder talking to camera, "storytime", educational tips, UGC-style reviews, and day-in-the-life.
- It must still deliver value and be truthful. Follow platform rules on labeling ads and paid partnerships.

## 8. Building the body of the ad

A practical sequence that draws on the techniques above:
1. **Hook:** desire or problem at the right awareness level.
2. **Identify:** "If you're someone who…" (they see themselves).
3. **Intensify:** the pain or the dream in vivid detail.
4. **Gradualize or concentrate:** why the usual solutions fail, building belief step by step.
5. **Mechanize:** how this product works differently.
6. **Verify:** proof (testimonials, numbers, demonstration).
7. **Redefine:** dissolve the main objection.
8. **Offer + risk killed + speed & ease.**
9. **One CTA** with a reason to act now.

Short ads compress or skip steps, but the logic stays the same.

## 9. Ethics

Schwartz's techniques make claims *believable*, so they must only be used on claims that are *true*. Never fabricate proof, testimonials, results, or scarcity. In regulated industries (health, aesthetics, supplements, finance, legal, education claims), avoid guaranteed outcomes and follow local advertising rules.
