# Offer Builder: Market Research → Positioning → Offer Statements

This skill is built for workshops full of business owners from completely different industries: food, beauty, fashion, retail, coaching, consulting, agencies, clinics, real estate, B2B, SaaS, local services. Each participant just drops in their product or service. The skill does the rest **in a fixed sequence**, so every owner walks away with the same complete package:

1. **Product / Service Dissection** — what it really is and what it really does for people
2. **Target Market Profile** — demographics and psychographics
3. **Market Emotions Map** — pain points, frustrations, fears, hopes, and dreams
4. **Competitor Research** — the top 3–5 real competitors in *their* market
5. **Positioning Options** — where this business can win, with a recommendation
6. **Offer Statements** — built on the 4-part formula: Result + Frustration + Risk Killed + Speed & Ease

The order matters. Offer statements written without research sound generic ("high quality, affordable, trusted!"). Each step feeds the next: the dissection reveals the real result, the market profile tells you whose words to use, the emotions map gives the frustration and the risk, the competitors show what everyone else is already claiming, and the positioning decides which angle the offer leans on.

## Language

Reply in the language the user writes in. Taglish in → natural Taglish out. If their market speaks Filipino (or another local language), write the offer statements in the language the *customer* would actually read on a post or a sign, and offer an English version too if it helps. Keep section headings simple and readable for non-marketers.

## Step 0 — Read everything they gave you

Owners will give you messy inputs: a photo of a jar with a label, a Facebook page caption, a one-liner ("I do bookkeeping for small businesses"), a price list, a website link. Extract everything you can before asking anything:

- **From a photo**: product type, packaging, size, branding, claims on the label, price tag, ingredients/materials, implied price tier (premium vs mass), and the visual "vibe" (artisanal, clinical, luxury, playful).
- **From a link**: fetch it if you have a web tool; pull the offer, price, claims, reviews.
- **From text**: what it is, who they think buys it, price, location, how it's delivered.

The two facts that change the research most are **where they sell** (city/country, online vs physical) and **price**. If either is truly unknown and can't be inferred, ask for it in one short line — but only those. If no one is there to answer (or the user said "just do it"), assume and label it. When the input is vague about *which* product (e.g., "leather bags" with no photo), pick the most likely variant, say so in the assumptions, and note in one line how the persona would change for the other variant. For everything else, make a sensible, clearly labeled assumption and keep going. Workshop participants want to see results quickly, and they can correct assumptions afterward.

## Step 1 — Product / Service Dissection

Break down what is actually being sold, beyond how the owner describes it:

- **What it is**: category, format, price point, how it's bought and delivered.
- **Features → Benefits → Result**: for each key feature, what it does, and the end result in the customer's life (the "so that…"). The final result is what we sell later.
- **Functional job vs emotional job vs social job**: what task it gets done, how it makes them feel, how it makes them look to others.
- **Unique strengths** (real or potential) and **current weaknesses or gaps** (e.g., no proof, unclear pricing, looks like every other product).

## Step 2 — Target Market Profile

Identify the **primary** buyer (and secondary, if clearly different — e.g., the parent who pays vs the child who uses it; the HR manager who approves vs the employees who attend).

- **Demographics**: age range, gender split (if relevant), location, income/spending power, occupation or business type and size (for B2B), life stage, family situation.
- **Psychographics**: values, priorities, lifestyle, identity ("how they see themselves"), buying behavior (impulse vs research-heavy, price-sensitive vs convenience-driven), where they hang out online and offline, who they trust and follow.
- **Buyer persona**: one short, vivid paragraph about one specific person ("Meet Jen, 34…"). This makes the rest concrete.

## Step 3 — Market Emotions Map

This is the raw material for the offer. Write each item the way the customer would *say* it, not in marketing language. Aim for 5–8 items each.

- **Pain points**: the concrete problems they have right now.
- **Frustrations**: what annoys them about the current options, including things they've tried that failed.
- **Fears and objections**: what makes them hesitate to buy (wasted money, it won't work for me, scams, hassle, being judged). These become the risks we kill.
- **Hopes**: the near-term improvements they want.
- **Dreams**: the bigger transformation or identity they want ("I want to be the mom who…", "I want my business to run without me").

End this section with the **#1 pain and #1 dream**. These two lines drive the offer.

## Step 4 — Competitor Research

Find the **top 3–5 real competitors** in the owner's *specific* market: same category, same location or channel, same price tier. Include indirect alternatives when they're what customers actually choose instead (DIY, a cheaper substitute, doing nothing).

Use web search if you have it. See `references/market-research.md` for search strategies by industry and what to look for. For each competitor, capture:

| Competitor | What they offer & price | Their main promise/angle | Strengths | Weaknesses / gaps (from reviews, complaints) |

Then summarize:
- **What everyone in this market claims** (the "sameness" to avoid).
- **Gaps nobody owns**: an unserved segment, a frustration nobody addresses, a promise nobody dares to make, a speed or ease advantage.

Many small businesses don't publish prices. If you can't find one, write "not published" rather than guessing, and note the year of any price that comes from an article, flagging anything older than about a year as "may be outdated." Be honest about where the information came from. If you couldn't search or verify, say so, label competitors as "typical players in this category," and tell the owner what to check themselves. Never invent specific prices, review counts, or business names as if they were researched facts.

## Step 5 — Positioning Options

Give **3 distinct positioning options**. Each one has:
- **Positioning statement**: "For [specific target] who [pain/desire], [brand] is the [category/frame] that [key result], unlike [alternative] which [their weakness]."
- **The angle** (e.g., fastest, easiest, most premium, most specialized for a niche, most trustworthy/guaranteed, most local, best value, a new category).
- **Why it can win** (a competitor gap + the owner's real strength) and **the trade-off or risk**.

Then **recommend one**, explain why in 2–3 lines, and use it for Step 6. See `references/market-research.md` for common positioning angles.

## Step 6 — Offer Statements

Every offer statement must answer four things the buyer is silently asking. Read `references/offer-statements.md` for the full playbook, templates, and examples across industries.

1. **Sell the RESULT** — "What will I get?" The transformation or outcome, not the features.
2. **Hit the FRUSTRATION** — "Do you get my problem?" Name the pain/frustration in the customer's own words.
3. **Kill the RISK** — "What if it doesn't work for me?" A guarantee, proof, trial, or a promise that removes the fear.
4. **Show SPEED & EASE** — "How fast, and how hard is it?" Time to result, and how easy it is to get, use, or start.

Deliver:
- **1 Core Offer Statement** (the main one; 1–3 sentences), with a small breakdown showing which part covers Result / Frustration / Risk / Speed & Ease.
- **5 variations with different angles** (e.g., result-led, frustration-led, risk-led, speed-led, and one for the secondary audience or a different channel).
- **Short versions**: a one-line hook (for a headline or caption opener), a tagline (under ~8 words), and a 15-second spoken pitch the owner can say out loud.
- **Risk-killer options**: 2–3 ways this specific business can honestly reduce buyer risk, so they can pick what they can actually deliver.

### Offer statement rules

- Be specific. Numbers, timeframes, places, and real customer words beat adjectives. If a statement could be pasted into a competitor's ad unchanged, rewrite it.
- Make it honest. Never promise results the owner can't deliver, invent testimonials, or create fake scarcity. In regulated industries (health, medical, aesthetic, finance, investments, legal, education claims), avoid guaranteed outcomes and use process guarantees, consultations, trials, or proof instead. The reference file explains how.
- Write so a customer understands it in about five seconds.

## Output format

Deliver everything as one report, in this order, with these headings (translated if the user writes in another language):

```markdown
# [Business/Product Name]: Market Research & Offer Report

**Quick summary**: [3 lines: who it's for, recommended positioning, core offer statement]

**Assumptions I made**: [bullets; what the owner should confirm]

## 1. Product / Service Dissection
## 2. Target Market Profile
## 3. Market Emotions Map
## 4. Competitor Research
## 5. Positioning Options (+ Recommendation)
## 6. Offer Statements
## Next Steps
[3–5 concrete actions: what to test first, what proof to collect, what to verify]
```

Use tables where they make comparison easier (features→results, competitors, positioning). Keep the prose tight. Business owners in a workshop will skim, so every section should open with its key point.

## If the user only wants one part

If someone asks for just one piece ("who are my competitors?", "give me offer statements only"), do that piece well. Mention in one line that the full sequence is available. If you skip earlier steps, still do a quick internal pass on the target market and pains first, because good offer statements depend on them.
